CERVICAL CANCER — FACTUAL REFERENCE

Overview
Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer in women globally, with approximately 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths in 2020. It occurs in the cells of the cervix — the lower part of the uterus that connects to the vagina. Virtually all cervical cancers are caused by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV). Crucially, cervical cancer is one of the most preventable and curable cancers when detected early and treated appropriately. The WHO has launched a global strategy to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem (target: < 4 per 100,000 women per year).

Causation — Human Papillomavirus (HPV)
HPV is a very common sexually transmitted infection — most sexually active men and women will be infected at some point. The majority of HPV infections clear spontaneously, but persistent infection with high-risk HPV types causes cervical cancer. High-risk HPV types 16 and 18 are responsible for approximately 70% of all cervical cancers and are targeted by all licensed HPV vaccines. Other high-risk types include 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58. HPV infection progresses through precancerous cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) stages before developing into invasive cancer — typically over 10-15 years.

Types of Cervical Cancer
Squamous cell carcinoma: Most common type — accounts for 70-80% of cervical cancers. Arises from squamous cells covering the outer part of the cervix (ectocervix). Caused predominantly by HPV 16 and 18. Adenocarcinoma: Accounts for 20-25% of cervical cancers. Arises from glandular cells lining the cervical canal. Increasingly common, particularly in younger women. Adenosquamous carcinoma: Mixed type. Other rare types: clear cell, neuroendocrine.

Risk Factors
HPV infection (necessary cause): HPV 16 and 18 most oncogenic. Multiple sexual partners and early age of first intercourse increase HPV exposure risk. Immunosuppression: HIV infection increases risk of HPV persistence and cervical cancer by 4-5 times. People living with HIV should receive more frequent cervical screening. Smoking: Increases risk of cervical cancer in HPV-infected women. Long-term use of oral contraceptives (>5 years): Small increase in risk. Multiple pregnancies. Lack of access to cervical screening. Sexually transmitted co-infections (chlamydia, herpes simplex).

Prevention — HPV Vaccination
HPV vaccination is the primary prevention strategy for cervical cancer. Three licensed HPV vaccines are available: Cervarix (bivalent: HPV 16, 18), Gardasil (quadrivalent: HPV 6, 11, 16, 18), Gardasil-9 (nonavalent: HPV 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58). WHO recommends HPV vaccination for girls aged 9-14 years (primary target) before sexual debut. Catch-up vaccination for young women up to age 26. Some guidelines recommend vaccination for women up to 45 and for men. Gardasil-9 prevents approximately 90% of HPV-related cervical cancers. Vaccination programs in countries like Australia, UK, and Nordic countries have dramatically reduced HPV infections, genital warts, and cervical cancer precursors. Important: Vaccination does NOT replace cervical screening — vaccinated women still require regular screening.

Prevention — Cervical Screening
Cervical screening (Pap smear/cytology): Detects precancerous changes (CIN) in cervical cells before they progress to cancer. Recommended every 3 years for women aged 21-65 (cytology alone). HPV testing: Primary HPV testing is now the preferred screening strategy in many countries. Detects high-risk HPV before cellular abnormalities develop. Recommended every 5 years for women aged 25-65. Co-testing (HPV + cytology): Recommended every 5 years (ages 30-65). Regular screening reduces cervical cancer incidence and mortality by approximately 70-80%. VIA (Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid): Low-cost screening method used in low-resource settings — trained health workers apply acetic acid and visually inspect the cervix.

Symptoms
Early cervical cancer may be asymptomatic. Symptoms when present: abnormal vaginal bleeding (between periods, after sexual intercourse, or after menopause), unusual vaginal discharge (watery, bloody, or foul-smelling), pelvic pain or pain during intercourse. Advanced disease: leg pain, back pain, swelling of one leg, difficulty urinating or defecating, fistulae (abnormal connections between vagina and bladder or rectum).

Diagnosis and Staging
Colposcopy and cervical biopsy: Confirm diagnosis of CIN or invasive cancer. Staging: Clinical staging using FIGO (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) classification. MRI (pelvis): Best for assessing local tumor extent, parametrial involvement, lymph nodes. CT or PET-CT: For detecting lymph node and distant metastases. Staging: Stage I (confined to cervix), Stage II (beyond cervix but not to pelvic wall), Stage III (to pelvic wall/lower vagina or causes hydronephrosis), Stage IV (bladder/rectum or distant metastases).

Treatment
Stage IA1 (microinvasive): LEEP (loop electrosurgical excision procedure), cold knife conization, or simple hysterectomy — fertility-sparing options available. Stage IB1-IIA (early locally confined): Radical hysterectomy + pelvic lymph node dissection (surgical), OR Definitive chemoradiation (equivalent oncologic outcomes). Stage IB3-IVA (locally advanced): Concurrent chemoradiation — cisplatin-based chemotherapy + external beam radiation + brachytherapy. This is the standard of care and achieves cure in a significant proportion. Stage IVB (distant metastasis): Systemic chemotherapy — cisplatin + paclitaxel ± bevacizumab. Immunotherapy: Pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) approved in combination with chemotherapy ± bevacizumab for persistent, recurrent, or metastatic cervical cancer with PD-L1 expression. Tisotumab vedotin: Antibody-drug conjugate for recurrent/metastatic cervical cancer.

Key Statistics
- Cervical cancer is the 4th most common cancer in women globally (2020).
- 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths in 2020.
- Over 85% of cervical cancer deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).
- In many high-income countries with screening programs, cervical cancer rates have fallen by over 80%.
- 5-year survival: Stage I ~90%, Stage II ~60%, Stage III ~35%, Stage IV ~10%.
- HPV vaccination is estimated to prevent up to 90% of cervical cancers.
- WHO's 90-70-90 targets: 90% of girls vaccinated by age 15, 70% of women screened by age 35 and 45, 90% of women with cervical disease treated.
